# Economist Radio Style

The register for the briefing script. Distilled on July 31st 2026 from the Economist style-guide plugin (`/Users/Shared/projects/oss/economist-style-guide-plugin`, MIT) and from The Economist's own corpus study of AI prose ("How to spot AI writing", July 30th 2026), which measured 1.2m words of LLM rewrites against the paper's originals. The banned-patterns list is empirical and dated: models change with every update, so treat it as a snapshot of mid-2026, not doctrine.

**Precedence.** The TTS pronunciation rules in SKILL.md always win. Spell out numbers, currencies and dates for the ear: "three point two billion dollars", "February eighth". The print guide's rules on figures, dates, punctuation and headings do not apply here. A listener cannot hear orthography.

**The target voice** is the paper's radio register, not its printed page: a presenter talking to one intelligent listener. Think The Intelligence. Authority carried lightly, a dry wit kept on a short leash, and no pomposity anywhere. The presenter speaks in the first person where a view is genuinely theirs, above all in the editorial.

## Write like Orwell talks

1. Short words. Saxon beats Latin: "use" not "utilise", "buy" not "purchase", "about" not "approximately", "help" not "facilitate".
2. Active voice. Name who did what: "the committee published the report".
3. Cut every word that does no work. If the sentence survives without it, it was dead weight.
4. Everyday English over jargon. If a phrase would sit comfortably in a press release, it is doing nothing in a script.
5. One idea per sentence. The listener cannot re-read.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say something barbarous.

## House habits (the audible Economist)

- **People.** Full name on first mention, honorific and surname after: "Janet Yellen... Ms Yellen". Job titles stay lower case and follow the name: "Jerome Powell, chairman of the Federal Reserve", never "Chairman Powell". Historical figures and the dead go bare: "Keynes", "Napoleon".
- **Institutions are singular.** "The government is", "Apple has", "Labour says".
- **Quote people.** Let the chancellor, the analyst, the dissident speak in their own words, and attribute *before* the quote so the listener knows whose voice is coming: "As Ms Reeves put it: ...". "Said" is the neutral verb. "Claimed", "admitted" and "insisted" all editorialise; use them only on purpose.
- **Anchor every number.** Give the comparison: "up from", "twice what it was", "its lowest since twenty nineteen". Round hard. Precision the ear cannot hold is noise.
- **Attribute views to named holders.** "Critics argue" is a weasel; "opposition MPs say the plan will cost two billion pounds more" is reporting.
- **Confidence without dogma.** State what is known plainly. Hedge only genuine uncertainty, and hedge once: "may", never "may potentially".
- **Adjective rationing.** Nouns and verbs carry the sentence. Cut intensifiers ("very", "really", "extremely") and evaluative padding ("impressive", "alarming", "stunning"). Give the fact; let the listener gasp.
- **Wit stays dry.** Understatement, an unexpected juxtaposition, a raised eyebrow in word choice. Never sarcasm, never a joke leaning on a tragedy.

## Banned patterns (the AI tells, mid-2026 edition)

These are the measured fingerprints of LLM prose. The briefing must not have them.

- **The overused words**: "significant", "increasingly", "consequences", "crucial", "key", "major", "massive", "ongoing", "iconic". Say the amount, or cut.
- **Nominalisations**: prefer the verb. "The expansion of trade" is "trade expanded"; "the implementation of the policy" is "the policy began".
- **Pretentious diction**: rare and scientific words where plain ones exist ("interdependence", "methodology", "parameter"). If a shorter word carries the meaning, the long one is costume.
- **The rhetorical tics**: "not X but Y", "not only... but also", and the rule of three. One good reason beats three engineered ones. Claude is the worst measured offender here, and this script is written by a Claude. Overcorrect.
- **The "and"-chain**: sentences glued together with "and" rolling past twenty-five words. Full stops are free.
- **Uniform rhythm**: paragraph after paragraph of evenly long sentences. Interrupt them. A four-word sentence lands.
- **Nobody quoted**: bots paraphrase everything; humans let people talk. See House habits — this rule pays off at writing time, when the source articles and their quotes are in hand.
- **Em-dashes**: still a Claude signature. Prefer commas and full stops; the TTS phrases them better anyway.

Field note, August 1st 2026: on the first script written under these rules, every news section came out clean while the editorial carried five not-X-but-Y scaffolds in four hundred words. The tic returns precisely where reporting turns to opinion. After drafting the editorial, sweep it again for these patterns before saving.

## Sound and shape (audio-only rules)

- Average sentence twelve to eighteen words, with wide variation around that. After two long sentences, write a short one.
- No parentheticals. If an aside matters, give it its own sentence.
- Transitions are turns of thought, not signposts. Prefer the logic doing the work over "moreover", "furthermore", "speaking of which".
- One rhetorical question per episode at most, best spent on the pivot into the editorial.
- Each section opens concrete: a fact, a scene, a number that surprises. Never a definition, never scene-setting fluff, never "in today's fast-paced world".
- Endings land on a specific: an image, a quote, a number. Never "only time will tell".

## Word rulings that matter on air

A crisis is a turning point, not any difficulty. To refute is to disprove; mere disagreement is to reject. To decimate is not to destroy. A dilemma has exactly two bad options. Epicentres belong to earthquakes. "Literally" means literally. Nothing is "very unique". And "whilst" is print-room fussiness; say "while".
